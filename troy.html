The whole cycle, in nine acts

Each act is short. Read in order the first time — the acts set up debts, bargains and tricks that pay off much later, and the little Threads tracker (bottom-right) lights up when a payoff lands.

Act 1

The Rigged Choice

A wedding, an uninvited guest, and a question with no safe answer.

Start with the gods. Zeus has decided the earth needs a great war — one lost poem says the point was to thin out the age of heroes. The mechanism he permits is small: a wedding invitation list with one name missing.

At the wedding of the sea-goddess Thetis to the mortal Peleus, Eris — Strife — arrives uninvited and rolls a golden apple into the feast, inscribed for the fairest. Hera, Athena and Aphrodite all claim it. Zeus, far too shrewd to judge three goddesses, dumps the verdict on a mortal herdsman named Paris.

Paris is more than he looks. He is a Trojan prince, abandoned at birth because of a prophecy that he would one day burn Troy, and raised among herdsmen. He has a lover, Oenone.

The goddesses do not argue their case; they bid. Hera offers empire. Athena offers victory in war. Aphrodite offers the most beautiful woman in the world. Paris picks desire — and abandons Oenone.

Notice the trap: this is a character test with no safe answer. Whichever goddess he crowns, the other two walk away humiliated — and become Troy's sworn enemies. The city's fate is sealed before anyone has mentioned love.

Why it matters

The war's first cause is not a runaway queen; it is divine politics — two of heaven's three most powerful goddesses now want Troy destroyed.

Act 2

The Oath

How one elopement drafts every king in Greece.

Aphrodite's prize has a name: Helen — and she is already queen of Sparta, married to King Menelaus. Paris arrives in Sparta as a guest, and leaves with her.

Rewind some years. Every powerful king in Greece had courted Helen, and her family feared the losers would start a war. So one clever suitor — Odysseus — invented the Oath of Tyndareus: all suitors swear to defend her marriage, whoever wins it. Now that oath detonates. It drafts every Greek king into Menelaus's war.

There is colder fuel under the romance: Troy is rich because it taxes the shipping that passes its straits. A war of honour that might also crack open the wealthiest toll-gate in the region finds many volunteers.

And Helen herself? Versions differ, and the ancients knew it. Some say she went willingly; some say she was taken. One tradition insists a phantom Helen went to Troy — while the real one sat out the entire war in Egypt.

Why it matters

A private wrong becomes everyone's war because of a legal trick — and the man who invented it will pay for it with twenty years of his life.

Next: rounding up the heroes — including two who really do not want to go. Act 3 ↓

Act 3

The Coalition

Three kinds of power, two draft-dodgers, one unbearable price.

The coalition runs on three kinds of power. Agamemnon, Menelaus's brother, holds positional power: the richest king in Greece, a hundred ships, the wronged family's claim. Achilles holds merit power: the best warrior alive. Odysseus holds intellect power: only twelve ships from rocky Ithaca, but he is the indispensable fixer every plan will need.

Achilles carries a prophecy shaped like a bargain: go to Troy and live a short, blazing, famous life — or stay home for a long, comfortable, forgotten one. He chooses fame. Hold that choice; the story will come back for it.

Both stars dodge the draft. Odysseus feigns madness, plowing a field and sowing it with salt — until his infant son is set down in front of the plow, and he swerves. Achilles is hidden in a royal household, disguised as a girl — until he alone grabs a spear planted among gifts.

The armada gathers at Aulis — and the wind dies. The price of sailing proves unbearable: Agamemnon sacrifices his own daughter, Iphigenia. The fleet gets its wind.

Mark this moment. It is a debt — and in this story, debts are always repaid.

Why it matters

Rank, merit and brains are welded into one army that already resents itself — and at Aulis the expedition takes on a blood debt.

Act 4

Ten Years of Siege

Not incompetence — geometry.

Ten years is not incompetence. Troy's walls were built by gods and cannot be stormed. Allies keep streaming in from across Asia Minor. And the gods themselves fight on both sides: Aphrodite, Apollo and Ares for Troy; Hera, Athena and Poseidon for the Greeks.

So the Greeks wage the unglamorous war instead: raiding the towns around Troy, stripping the coast, strangling the supply lines that feed the city.

Inside the walls, Troy's assembly debates simply giving Helen back and ending it. The elder Antenor argues yes. Paris refuses. Pride wins the vote.

Why it matters

A city that cannot be stormed can only be starved or tricked — remember that when a wooden horse appears.

Next: in year ten the Greek army nearly destroys itself. That slice is Homer's Iliad. Act 5 ↓

Act 5This slice = Homer's Iliad

The Wrath of Achilles

A few weeks of year ten — and the whole of Homer's first poem.

Everything in this act happens in a few weeks of year ten. This thin slice — not the whole war — is what Homer's Iliad actually tells.

Agamemnon must give back a captive woman to her father, a priest. He recoups the loss by seizing Briseis — Achilles's own prize. Understand what this is: rank publicly demoting merit; the commander showing the best warrior exactly where power sits.

But Achilles is not a subordinate. He is a sovereign king who cannot be ordered — so he withdraws himself and his men from the war entirely. The result is immediate: Hector, Troy's great defender, drives the Greeks back onto the beach, to their burning ships.

Patroclus, Achilles's beloved companion, cannot watch it. (What were they to each other? Homer leaves the bond ambiguous; later Greeks read them as lovers; it has been debated for 2,500 years — and the debate itself is the honest answer.) He borrows Achilles's armor to drive the Trojans back, overreaches — and Hector kills him.

Achilles returns to battle in annihilating grief. He kills Hector — then drags the corpse behind his chariot for days.

Then the tradition's most humane scene. Old King Priam slips into the enemy camp by night, kisses the hands that killed his son, and begs for Hector's body. Achilles weeps with him — and relents.

The Iliad ends there, at Hector's funeral. Read that again: inside Homer's poem there is no wooden horse, no fall of Troy, no death of Achilles. Those belong to other, later parts of the tradition.

Why it matters

The Iliad is not "the war" — it is a study of wrath and grief a few weeks long, and it ends with two enemies weeping together.

Act 6

The Fall

Cunning finishes what force could not — and goes too far.

The prophecy collects. Paris kills Achilles with an arrow guided by Apollo. (Note: the invulnerable "heel" is a later, non-Homeric addition — in the older telling, a god's aim is explanation enough.)

Philoctetes shoots Paris. Dying, Paris crawls back to Oenone — the lover he abandoned for Helen — and begs her to heal him. She refuses. He dies; in grief, she hangs herself.

Achilles's divine armor is put up as a prize for the army's best man. It is awarded to Odysseus — over the giant Ajax, the strongest Greek left alive. Ajax kills himself in shame. The tradition has delivered its verdict: from here on, cunning outranks force.

Odysseus devises the wooden horse. The Greeks burn their camp and pretend to sail for home, leaving one man behind — Sinon — to sell the lie.

Troy's truth-tellers are silenced on cue. The priest Laocoon warns against the horse and is strangled by sea serpents. Cassandra — cursed to prophesy truly and never be believed — is ignored as always. Troy breaches its own wall to bring the horse inside.

Night falls, and the trap closes. In the sack, old Priam is butchered at his own altar; Cassandra is dragged from Athena's temple. The sacrilege matters: the gods now turn against the victors.

Why it matters

Troy falls to a lie in wooden form — and the winners' cruelty in victory costs them the gods' favour on the way home.

Next: the homecomings — where the debt from Aulis is finally repaid. Act 7 ↓

Act 7

Homecomings

Nobody simply sails home.

Storms scatter the victorious fleet across the sea. For the Greeks, winning the war turns out to be the easy part.

Menelaus raises his sword over Helen — and drops it. He then wanders for years, by way of Egypt, before finally bringing her home to Sparta and a quiet, unglamorous reconciliation.

Agamemnon returns home in triumph — and is murdered in his bath by his queen, Clytemnestra. Ten years late, the debt for Iphigenia is repaid in full.

Their son Orestes avenges his father — and vengeance breeds vengeance, until Athena ends the blood feud by inventing something entirely new: the jury trial. The cycle of payback is handed to a courtroom.

Why it matters

The aftermath completes the moral ledger: the man who sacrificed his daughter does not get to enjoy his victory.

Act 8This slice = Homer's Odyssey

The Cursed Voyage

Not lost — cursed, precisely.

First, correct the famous misconception: Odysseus is not "lost at sea for ten years." He is cursed — and the curse is precise. Roughly three years of catastrophe, then seven years as a prisoner.

The curse has an author. Trapped in a cave by the cyclops Polyphemus, who eats his men, Odysseus tells the giant his name is Nobody — then blinds him and escapes hidden under the sheep. When Polyphemus roars that "Nobody" has attacked him, no help comes.

Then the fatal flex: from his ship, Odysseus shouts his real name. Polyphemus is a son of Poseidon, and he prays an exact curse: let this man come home late, broken, alone, on a stranger's ship, to a house in chaos. The rest of the poem fulfills that prayer line by line.

The stops, in order: the Cicones raid; the Lotus-Eaters, whose fruit erases the longing for home; the Cyclops; Aeolus's bag of winds, opened by the greedy crew within sight of Ithaca; the Laestrygonian cannibals, who destroy eleven of the twelve ships.

Circe turns his men to pigs until, armed with a divine herb, he beats her spell. Then the Underworld. His mother is among the dead — she died of grief, waiting. Achilles's ghost recants the great bargain: better a living farmhand, he says, than king of all the dead. And Agamemnon's ghost gives hard advice: go home in disguise.

Homeward arithmetic: past the Sirens, tied to the mast; then a commander's choice between Scylla and Charybdis — lose six men to the monster, or risk the whole ship in the whirlpool. He loses the six. Then the starving crew eats the Sun god's cattle, and Zeus sinks the ship. One man survives.

Calypso keeps that one man on her island for seven years — and offers him the ultimate prize: immortality, with her, forever. He refuses it, for a mortal wife and a rocky island. It is the exact inverse of Achilles's bargain. At last the Phaeacians ferry him home, asleep, on one of their own ships.

Why it matters

Twenty years after Aulis the curse is complete: late, broken, alone, on a stranger's ship — to a house in chaos.

Act 9

Ithaca

A beggar, a bow, and a bed that cannot be moved.

The chaos has a number: 108 suitors — young nobles occupying the palace, eating the estate bare, each demanding that Penelope choose one of them as husband and king.

Why can't she just refuse? Count her prison bars: there is no proof Odysseus is dead; she has no force able to expel armed nobles; remarriage would strip her son Telemachus of his inheritance — and the suitors are plotting to murder the boy anyway. Every exit is blocked.

So she filibusters. She will choose a husband, she announces, when she finishes weaving a funeral shroud — and every night she secretly unpicks the day's work. The trick holds for three years, until a maid betrays her.

Odysseus lands — and takes the ghost's advice, entering his own palace disguised as a beggar. His dying dog Argos knows him instantly, with strength left for a single wag of the tail. His old nurse knows him by a boar scar from his youth.

Penelope announces a contest: she will marry whichever man can string Odysseus's great bow and shoot an arrow through twelve axe heads. Suitor after suitor fails. Only the shabby beggar can do it.

He turns the bow on the suitors. With Telemachus at his side, he kills all 108 — in the world of the poem, justice for attempted murder and years of pillage. The disloyal maids are hanged too. The myth does not sanitize its homecoming, and neither should we.

One test left — and it is hers. Penelope orders their marriage bed moved out of the bedroom for the stranger. He erupts: impossible — he built that bed himself around a living olive tree; it cannot be moved. Only her husband could know that. Twenty years end.

Athena imposes peace on the feuding families. And a prophecy grants Odysseus what Achilles never wanted: a long life, and a gentle death far from the sea, in old age.

Why it matters

The epic ends not with a battle but with a marriage of equals — the bed test is cunning recognizing cunning — and all three long threads close.

05 · Myth vs Movie

What Troy (2004) changed

If the Brad Pitt film is your mental picture of this story, these are the edits to undo. Tap each card's toggle to compare.

Agamemnon's death

In the filmAgamemnon is killed during the sack of Troy.

In the mythHe survives the war and returns home in triumph — where Clytemnestra murders him in his bath. His death is the whole point of the homecoming story, not a battlefield footnote.

Menelaus's death

In the filmHector kills Menelaus mid-war.

In the mythMenelaus survives, raises his sword over Helen, drops it — and after years of wandering via Egypt brings her home to a quiet reconciliation.

Achilles at the horse

In the filmAchilles is alive for the sack, dying inside Troy.

In the mythParis's arrow — guided by Apollo — kills Achilles before the horse exists. The horse is built precisely because the Greeks' best warrior is gone and force has failed.

The gods, deleted

In the filmNo gods appear; the war is purely human politics.

In the mythRemove the gods and the logic breaks: the apple starts the war, gods hold the walls, Apollo guides the fatal arrow, and Poseidon's curse shapes the entire journey home.

Patroclus the "young cousin"

In the filmPatroclus is a boyish cousin under Achilles's protection.

In the mythHe is Achilles's beloved companion — the one person whose death unmakes him. Homer leaves the bond unlabeled; antiquity debated it for centuries. "Cousin" is the one reading with no ancient support.

Ajax's end

In the filmHector kills Ajax in battle.

In the mythAjax outlives Hector. He dies by his own hand, in shame, after Achilles's armor is awarded to Odysseus — the tradition's verdict that cunning now outranks force.

Nolan's The Odyssey (2026)

The filmBroadly faithful to the poem, told nonlinearly.

The poemNonlinear is Homer's own structure: the Odyssey starts near the end, with the wanderings delivered as a flashback. A jumbled telling is the authentic one.
